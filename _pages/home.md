Almost every cell in your body carries the same genes. A nerve cell and a
skin cell differ because each uses a different set of them. To use a gene,
a cell copies it into a messenger RNA (mRNA), which carries the
instructions for making a protein. Which mRNAs a cell keeps, and for how
long, helps decide what the cell becomes. Our lab studies the RNAs and
proteins that make these choices. MicroRNAs are tiny RNAs that switch off
particular mRNAs, and we found out how cells make them. In stem cells, for
example, a protein called LIN28 blocks a microRNA that would push them to
specialize. Each mRNA also ends in a long string of A's, its tail, which
works a bit like a timer. We found that adding U's to the tail marks an
mRNA for destruction, while a G slipped in among the A's protects it. Early
embryos use the U mark to clear out the mRNAs their mothers left them and
switch to their own. We also study how cells recognize RNA that comes from
viruses. What we learn helps us design mRNA medicines, like mRNA vaccines,
that last longer inside cells.
